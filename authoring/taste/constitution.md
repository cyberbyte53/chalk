# Chalk constitution

Twelve principles every lesson is made and judged by. Each carries its reason: when a case isn't covered, reason from the why. A student here is in the class the book is for, reading on a phone, often in their second language.

1. **Teach the idea before you use it.** Every word, symbol and label (including marks drawn in a prop, like F and 2F) is defined before anything leans on it; everyday words (ray, substance, trait) get a one-line recap. *Why: one undefined word and the student stops understanding everything after it — and they rarely say so.*

2. **Start from what they know, then go one step.** Each page opens from the question the last page left ("7/30 never ends. Do its digits repeat?"); each lesson opens from the last lesson. *Why: learning is a chain; a page with no link to the one before reads as noise.*

3. **Numbers before letters.** Open with a worked numeric case (12 and 144), then write the general statement in symbols, with the same steps. *Why: symbols are a summary of cases the student has seen; without the cases there is nothing to summarise.*

4. **Choose the order the idea needs, but always let them touch it.** Where a result is intuitive to discover (sharing sweets in rounds → the division lemma), let them find it, then state it. Where it's a law best said plainly (the laws of reflection), state it, then hand over the prop so they see it hold. Either way the student does something with it before moving on. *Why: discovery makes a result believed, but forcing discovery onto a law just makes them guess; what both need is the student seeing it work with their own hands.*

5. **Every step has its true reason.** Rules, proof lines and worked-example steps each say why ("the radius meets the circle at right angles, so this is the normal"; "we want 4m, so square 2"). The reason must be the real one. *Why: steps without reasons can only be copied, and a false reason has to be unlearned.*

6. **Show, don't describe.** Mechanisms and comparisons get a picture or a demonstration; maths is written as maths (a sentence, then the equation on its own line), not described in words. *Why: a child reading a paragraph about angles builds a worse picture than the one we could have drawn for them.*

7. **One idea per page, one model per chapter.** Pages hold one idea and scroll; a chapter carries a few props reused everywhere rather than many one-offs. *Why: each new model costs attention to learn; reuse spends that attention on the subject.*

8. **Questions test the subject, not reading or guessing.** A question names its object and makes sense alone; the right option never stands out; wrong options are real mistakes students make. *Why: a question the student gets right by elimination, or wrong by misreading, tells us nothing and teaches less.*

9. **A reason to care, and company while learning.** A chapter has one goal a student would care about, from their own world (home, school, festivals), carried by recurring characters the student learns alongside; its facts and timeline never contradict. *Why: correct but disconnected pages read as bland, learning with someone beats learning alone, and children notice every inconsistency.*

10. **Plain, calm, exact.** Short sentences, plain teacher language, one name per thing used everywhere (text and props alike), headings that name the result or ask the real question. White space, no flash. *Why: the student's attention is the scarcest thing on the screen.*

11. **The whole source, at the class level.** Keep everything the source teaches, including removed sections, and nothing beyond it; when in doubt, cut. Proofs use the standard tools and notation of that class, not just the picture. *Why: students are examined on the source; extra depth is extra load.*

12. **Read it as the student.** Before anything is called done, read every sentence literally as a student of that class, on a phone. *Why: the builder knows what they meant; only the student's reading counts.*
