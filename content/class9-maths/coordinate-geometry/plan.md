## Learning objectives

By the end, a student can: (1) locate a number on the number line; (2) say why a place in a plane needs two numbers from two perpendicular lines; (3) name the x-axis, y-axis, origin and the positive and negative directions; (4) name the quadrants, I to IV anticlockwise, and the coordinate plane; (5) define the x-coordinate (abscissa) and y-coordinate (ordinate) as signed distances from the axes; (6) read a point's coordinates; (7) know (x, 0), (0, y) and (0, 0); (8) tell a quadrant from the signs, and why; (9) plot a point, decimals included; (10) know (x, y) is an ordered pair.

## Lessons (in NCERT order)

1. **two-numbers-for-a-place** (3.1; Figs 3.1–3.4; Activity 1; Exercise 3.1 Q1–Q2). Discover → state.
   - Question: how do you say exactly where something is?
   - Hook: Sania's friend lives "on Street 2". Which house? The student taps along Street 2 and sees one number leaves many houses; street 2 *and* house 5 leaves one (H in Fig 3.1; P is street 7, house 4).
   - Then: the dot on the paper. "Near the left edge" fixes nothing; 5 cm from the left edge **and** 9 cm above the bottom fixes it (Fig 3.2). Two independent pieces of information, measured from two fixed lines.
   - The seating plan (Activity 1, Fig 3.3): the shaded desk is (5, 3), column first. Is it the same as (3, 5)? The student taps both and sees two different desks. Sonia at S(4, 1).
   - State: the position of anything in a plane needs two perpendicular reference lines. Descartes, lying in bed (Fig 3.4); the Cartesian system.
   - Practice: Exercise 3.1 Q1 (the table lamp, as a choice: which description fixes it), Q2 (the cross-streets: exactly one is (4, 3), exactly one is (3, 4), and they are different).
   - Props: `coordinatePlane` (grid: whole-number crossings in one quadrant, axis names from the lesson: street and house, column and row, cm from the left edge and from the bottom).
   - New terms: position, reference line.
2. **the-cartesian-plane** (3.2 to Fig 3.9; Exercise 3.2 Q1). State → see it hold.
   - Question: what if the fountain is in the middle, and prizes are on every side?
   - Learn: the number line again (origin, equal units, −r on the other side, Fig 3.5); two number lines X′X and Y′Y placed perpendicular, crossing at their zeros (Figs 3.6–3.8); the **x-axis**, the **y-axis**, the **origin** O; positive directions OX, OY and negative OX′, OY′; the axes cut the plane into four **quadrants**, numbered I to IV anticlockwise from OX (Fig 3.9); the Cartesian (coordinate, xy-) plane.
   - See it in life: the park map with the fountain at O; east is the positive x-axis, west the negative.
   - Practice: Exercise 3.2 Q1 (the names); tap the quadrant a spot on the map lies in.
   - Props: `coordinatePlane` (axes: the two number lines slide in and cross at zero; tap a quadrant to name it).
   - New terms: x-axis, y-axis, origin, quadrant.
3. **coordinates-of-a-point** (3.2 from Fig 3.10; Examples 1–2; Fig 3.13; the Remark; Exercise 3.2 Q2). Discover → state.
   - Question: how do we write a point down so there is no confusion?
   - Discover: drag the prize P around the map; dotted perpendiculars drop to both axes and their lengths show, with a sign (Fig 3.10: P is 4 from the y-axis and 3 from the x-axis; Q is 6 to the left and 2 down).
   - State: the **x-coordinate** (abscissa) is the signed distance from the y-axis, the **y-coordinate** (ordinate) the signed distance from the x-axis; written (x, y), x first, in brackets; P(4, 3), Q(−6, −2). (3, 4) is not (4, 3).
   - Then: Example 1 (B, M, L, S in Fig 3.11, one per quadrant); Example 2 (points on the axes, Fig 3.12, including E(2/3, 0)): every point on the x-axis is (x, 0), on the y-axis (0, y); the origin is (0, 0); the signs in each quadrant, and why (Fig 3.13): (+, +), (−, +), (−, −), (+, −). The Remark: x first is a convention the whole world keeps.
   - Practice: Example 1–2 as questions; Exercise 3.2 Q2 (Fig 3.14: read B, C, D's abscissa, H's ordinate, L, M; name the point at (−3, −5), (2, −4)).
   - Props: `coordinatePlane` (read: drag a point, perpendiculars and signed distances drawn; points named on the plane).
   - New terms: x-coordinate (abscissa), y-coordinate (ordinate), coordinates.
4. **plotting-points** (3.3; Figs 3.15–3.17; Examples 3–4; the Note; Activity 2; the Remark; Exercise 3.3 Q1–Q2). State → see it hold.
   - Question: given the coordinates, where is the point?
   - Learn: to plot (3, 5), start at O, go 3 along the x-axis (A), then 5 up, parallel to the y-axis: P (Fig 3.15). Q(5, −4) goes down, into quadrant IV.
   - See it hold: Example 3's eight points by the student's own steps; (5, 0) and (0, 5), (5, 2) and (2, 5) land in different places: the Note, (x, y) is an **ordered pair**, (x, y) ≠ (y, x) when x ≠ y, and equal when x = y. Example 4's table, with (0, −3.5) between grid lines.
   - Activity 2: the dice game at the mela stall: two dice, move (red, green) from where you stand, first to (10, 10) without overshooting.
   - The Remark: a time–distance graph is the same plane with axes named t and d.
   - Practice: Exercise 3.3 Q1 (quadrant or axis of five points, then plot them), Q2 (plot the table, with −1.25).
   - Finale: the hunt and the (5, 3) team.
   - Props: `coordinatePlane` (plot: step along x, then along y, place the dot; dice: roll and move).
   - New terms: plot, ordered pair.

## Props

One new prop carries the whole chapter (`library/catalogue.md` checked: nothing places a point in a plane; `@chalk/draw` has a one-dimensional `Axis` only).

| Prop | What it shows | Learner can | Used in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **coordinatePlane** (new, `@chalk/prop-coordinates`) | A square grid with two perpendicular number lines. Set by the lesson: the range (one quadrant, or −n to n), the axis names (street/house, column/row, x/y, t/d), named points, and what is shown: the axes sliding in to cross at zero, quadrant numbers, a point's perpendiculars to the axes with their signed lengths, its coordinates. Plotting: the learner steps from O along the x-axis, then parallel to the y-axis, and places the dot; half steps reach 2/3 and −3.5 where a source example needs them. Dice: two dice, the counter moves by (red, green), capped at (10, 10). Its state says where every point is, so questions are checked against it. | tap a crossing, a cell or a quadrant; drag a point; step along x and y and place a point; roll the dice | L1–L4, questions |
